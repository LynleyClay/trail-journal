# Your life expands and contracts in proportion to your courage

When I started the Appalachian Trail, I didn’t have a big reason for being out there. People always told me you need a “why” so you don’t quit. But the truth is, I just wanted to see if I could do it.

About a quarter of the way in, in southern Virginia, I met a woman named Murray-Ann. I was eating at a gas station Mexican restaurant when she came up and asked if I wanted to come over to her house. I said yes, which in hindsight sounds like every parent’s worst nightmare, but she seemed harmless enough.

Murray-Ann’s husband had thru-hiked the Appalachian Trail, and the community meant everything to him. After he passed away, hikers from all over reached out to share stories about how he’d helped them out on the trail. That’s when she realized how special the community was, and she decided to give back in his honor. That’s why she became a trail angel. That’s why she picked up strangers at gas stations and opened her home to people like me.

She couldn’t hike the trail herself anymore. So she found her own way to walk the miles. She started virtually hiking the Appalachian Trail, walking around her neighborhood, or up and down her hallway if it was raining, then crossing off that section on the map when she finished. One mile at a time. She finished her virtual thru-hike last year.

She couldn’t do it the way her husband did. So she found her own way.

While I was staying with her, I told her I wasn’t sure why I was on the trail, or what I was doing with my life. She told me something I’ve never forgotten.

“Your life expands and contracts in proportion to your courage.”

Then she told me that whenever she cooks, she always adds one new ingredient to the recipe. It doesn’t matter if it’s a family recipe passed down for generations or something out of a cookbook. She changes something every time. She does it to practice coloring outside the lines. Small acts of courage, repeated daily. That’s how you build the muscle.

I still think about that conversation. I went back to see her last year, and she hasn’t changed a bit.

I didn’t fully understand what she meant back then, and I definitely didn’t know how much that detour would end up mattering. But looking back, staying with Murray-Ann was one of the big pivotal moments of my hike. It’s where I started to trust that I was out there for a reason, and that if I took the risk and had a little courage, things would open up for me. And they did.
