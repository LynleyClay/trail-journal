# A friend named Trash

*One man’s trash is another man’s treasure.*

By the time I hit this stretch of the Appalachian Trail, everyone I knew had gotten off. All for different reasons, but at the end of the day, I was alone. It scared me, but I needed to make peace with it.

My first night solo camping, I spilled my chicken flavored ramen all over the ground. That night, a bear came to my campsite. I texted my parents at 11pm: “I’m by myself and I think there’s a bear outside my tent.” They texted back: “What do you want us to do about it?”

They were right. So I did the one brave thing any woman in her early 20s would do during her first solo bear encounter. I blasted my favorite One Direction song to scare it off. I sang every word while trembling in my tent, and by the time the song ended, the bear was gone. Plenty of girls can say One Direction saved their life, but I mean it. Literally.

It wasn’t just me who was scared. My parents were too. My dad started calling constantly, asking what my plan was. What I was going to do after this, because “you can’t hike forever.” First he was afraid I’d get hurt out there on my own. Then I hit a thousand miles, and the fear changed. Now he was worried I’d disappear down the hippie route and never come back to a normal life.

And I understood the fear, because up to that point my life had been a straight line. I went to school. I got a degree. I applied to grad school. I started hiking. Oops. And then I just kept hiking. From the outside it looked like I’d stopped making progress, like I’d stepped off the path and forgotten to get back on.

That was the headspace I was in when I met Trash.

I’d seen him once, briefly, at a shelter back in Virginia. One where you can order pizza from a local restaurant and they will deliver it to you. Then I met him for real in Harpers Ferry, West Virginia, the halfway point of the trail, right when I started seriously hiking alone.

We never really hiked together. He was more of a recurring character, a face I’d see in passing most days. Someone to have lunch with. Someone who made the trail feel a little less empty.

And Trash had lived the most interesting life of anyone I’d met.

He was a master of every modern hippie art form: hacky sack, slackline, and most famously, bike polo. He was a hitchhiker and a harmonica player. An engineer and a surfer. A part time ski instructor and a puppet maker. He wrote letters to spiritual retreat leaders and earned scholarships to go meditate and fast in the wilderness for weeks at a time.

He was only a few years older than me, and somehow he was already living ten lives at once.

And he did it all on his own terms. Half the time he wouldn’t even finish a trail. He’d hike the part he wanted, and if he felt like coming back for the rest later, he would. He’d follow whatever opportunity showed up, usually alone, not because he had a plan, but because he wanted to see what was there. The point was never crossing a finish line. It was learning something. Growing. Collecting one more experience.

I couldn’t get over it. I was out there partly because I had no idea what I was supposed to do with my life, and everyone kept asking me to name it. Trash had refused the whole question. He wasn’t an engineer who surfed on the weekends, or a ski instructor with a few hobbies. He was all of it, and he wasn’t in any rush to explain or rank a single part.

He showed me you don’t have to be afraid of what’s next. You can be so many things, and isn’t that exciting? A life doesn’t have to march toward one finish. It can just be the next thing you’re curious enough to go try.

What I couldn’t see yet was that stepping off the line wasn’t falling behind. Those detours, the ones that scared everybody, ended up teaching me more than the straight path ever had. They opened doors I didn’t even know existed.

But the bigger thing he gave me was quieter. I had spent that whole stretch terrified of being alone, sure I couldn’t do this without someone beside me. And somewhere in those weeks of running into Trash, then heading off by myself again, something changed. I stopped needing company to feel okay. I learned I could do this on my own. That being alone wasn’t a thing to survive until it ended. It was part of what I came for.

We only ever saw each other in passing. He still became one of my best friends.

I didn’t know it yet, but Trash was going to matter to my life in ways I never could have guessed. That’s a story for another time.
