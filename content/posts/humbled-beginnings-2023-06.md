# Out of my league

The Appalachian Trail taught me to be alone. By the time I finished, I didn’t just tolerate hiking by myself, I preferred it, and I had a huge head about it. I felt like I had crushed the AT, and I wanted something harder.

So I picked the Continental Divide Trail.

The CDT is the most dangerous and remote of the long trails. The one a lot of hikers are too intimidated to ever attempt. The weather window is brutal and narrow. It’s so remote that when a grizzly causes problems somewhere else in the country, they relocate it there. They call it the trail of solitude, because you can go weeks without seeing another person.

That was the whole point for me. I wanted to know what being alone that long would do to me. I wanted to break myself a little and find out what I was actually made of.

Then I showed up and met the people who hike the CDT.

These were not casual hikers. Everyone had these tiny five pound packs and moved like they were barely trying. On day one I met a girl named Rascal who mentioned, completely casually, that she was doing a 50 mile day.

I laughed. I thought she was joking. I had never heard of anyone hiking 50 miles in a day in my life.

She wasn’t joking. And looking back, the fact that I thought it was silly is hilarious, because I just had no frame of reference for what these people could do. Rascal would go on to hike a 108 mile day, town to town, across Wyoming. She led the crew that helped Tara Dower set the Appalachian Trail speed record. That is who I laughed at.

She wasn’t offended, thank god. She thought it was funny too, and we actually became friends and hung out in the next town. But that moment was the first crack in my confidence.

I met a guy named Coop the same way. Tiny pack, unbelievably fast, so cool. I saw him once and then never again, because he was just gone.

That was the crowd I had walked into. People in the top one percent of a sport most people don’t even know is a sport. The kind of hikers you would never know existed unless you happened to start on the same day, because within hours they blow past you and disappear up the trail.

And there I was. Same 24 pound pack I carried on the AT. Standing next to these people, I looked like a little kid who wandered onto the wrong trail. My great accomplishment was a small mark on their list. The head I had shown up with was deflating by the hour, and for the first time I started to wonder if I had shot way out of my league.

I started the Appalachian Trail secretly wanting to quit, with no idea how I was ever going to finish it. And by the end of day one on the CDT, I realized I was starting this one the exact same way.
